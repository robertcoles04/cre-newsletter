Top Story

Office CMBS Delinquencies Hit a Record

Fitch Ratings' overall U.S. CMBS delinquency rate rose to 3.60% in October from 3.46% in September, driven by a wave of new defaults led by office. The office delinquency rate reached an all-time high of 9.33%, beating the prior record.

Source: Connect CRE: Office CMBS Delinquencies Hit a Record

Why it matters When office loans inside these bonds stop paying, bondholders lose income and lenders grow wary, making it harder and pricier for any office owner to refinance.

Top Stories

Industry Sentiment Slips as Deals Stall

The Real Estate Roundtable's Q4 2026 Sentiment Index fell 10 points to 53, as higher interest rates and geopolitical uncertainty pushed investors to hold off on deals. The Current Index dropped 16 points to 47, even as property operations stayed healthy across most sectors.

Why it matters: sentiment surveys track how willing large investors are to buy and sell, and a drop means fewer transactions, which slows price discovery and leaves sellers waiting.

Source: Connect CRE: Industry Sentiment Slips as Deals Stall

A Korean Fund Warns an AI Credit Event Could Spread

A large South Korean fund said it plans to trim its real estate holdings and warned that a credit problem tied to AI lending could ripple into other markets. A credit event is when a borrower defaults or a loan sours badly enough to rattle investors.

Why it matters: if a major global investor pulls back from property, it removes a buyer from the market and can drag values lower for everyone still holding.

Source: Markets Group: A Korean Fund Warns an AI Credit Event Could Spread

Pending Home Sales Fell in October

Pending home sales dropped 4.7% in October, according to the National Association of Realtors. Pending sales count signed contracts, so they hint at where closings are headed over the next month or two.

Why it matters: softer housing demand pressures homebuilders and apartment owners, since would-be buyers stay put and for-sale listings sit longer.

Source: National Association of REALTORS®: Pending Home Sales Fell in October

Debt Markets

Higher borrowing costs could stall the rebound in CMBS issuance expected in 2027, according to CoStar. CMBS (commercial mortgage-backed securities) are bonds backed by pools of property loans, and when lenders and bond buyers demand higher yields, new deals get harder to pencil. CoStar

Why it matters: fewer new CMBS deals mean less fresh financing for owners who need to refinance loans coming due.

A $250 million CMBS loan on 101 Hudson Street, a 1.4 million-square-foot Jersey City office tower, was sent to special servicing after missing its October maturity. Special servicing is where troubled loans go to be worked out or headed toward foreclosure. Commercial Observer

Why it matters: another large office loan in trouble pushes lenders to tighten terms on similar buildings, raising costs for every office borrower.

Servicers have modified nearly $100 billion of CMBS loans since 2022, but one in five dollars from those fixes is delinquent again, Trepp reports. Modifying a loan means changing its terms to buy the borrower time. Trepp

Why it matters: if workouts keep failing, lenders may stop offering them, pushing more struggling owners toward foreclosure or sale.

A $140 million commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) loan tied to 123 William Street, a 27-story office tower in Manhattan's Financial District, has entered special servicing. Occupancy fell sharply, pushing the building below break-even, and its owners face bankruptcy risks. Special servicing means a troubled-loan specialist now handles the debt, according to Commercial Observer.

Why it matters: CMBS loans are bundled and sold to bond investors, so when a big office building stops earning enough to cover its debt, those investors can lose money and lenders grow warier of other shaky office towers.

Distress Watch: The Real Deal looks at a lender taking control of 12 distressed New York buildings and asks what a lender actually wants with a troubled portfolio. The Real Deal

Market Watch

Sun Belt

Dallas-Fort Worth developers are pushing industrial projects beyond the metro's established core as they chase cheaper land, Bisnow reports. DFW is already one of the nation's top warehouse markets, and the growth is spreading to outlying areas. Bisnow

Why it matters: cheaper land on the edges lets developers build warehouses at rents tenants can still afford, helping DFW keep winning logistics tenants.

West Coast

A San Diego investor paid $51 million for a Woodinville industrial park outside Seattle, according to The Business Journals. The out-of-state buyer stepped in for a suburban warehouse and flex-space complex. The Business Journals

Why it matters: out-of-state buyers paying up for industrial parks signals they still see steady demand for warehouse space around Seattle.

International

The Cerberus-backed company behind London's Battersea Glassmill tower is being liquidated after a failed planning bid and local opposition, Bisnow reports. The project was held in an SPV, a company set up to own one development. Bisnow

Why it matters: when a single-project company collapses, its lenders and investors can lose money, a reminder that failing to win planning approval can sink a development.

Term of the Day

Price per square foot

A building's sale price divided by its total size. It lets buyers compare deals of different sizes on equal footing. If a $10 million building holds 100,000 square feet, that is $100 per square foot. A nearby building selling for more per foot is pricier for the same amount of space.
